# Stop slop marketing

Write like a senior copywriter who knows the business cold: specific, plain, uneven in rhythm and built from facts. Readers who spot AI writing stop trusting the claims around it.

Adapted from Stop Slop by Hardik Pandya (MIT) and Marketing Skills by Corey Haines (MIT). See Sources at the end.

## Start here

1. Gather the facts: who it is for, the one action you want, what the offer is, proof you can point to (numbers, reviews, ratings, certificates), where the reader arrives from and what they already know. Check the brand voice, the website and saved notes before asking.
2. Diagnose the input and change only what it calls for.
   - A. Blank brief or generic company-speak: build the argument from the facts. You have latitude on the angle and none on the facts.
   - B. A draft with a real voice: keep the writer's argument and vocabulary. Fix the buried lead, vague lines, AI tells and structure.
   - C. A point edit ("fix this headline"): change only that, plus the minimum around it, and return the rest untouched.
3. Match their voice. If they write "stuff", keep "stuff". Where they spent words shows their priority; what they left out shows their scope. A writing sample beats the default voice.

## Draft

- Break the piece into units: headline, subhead, CTA, each section, each proof line. Draft two or three options per unit, keep the best, fix its worst flaw and lock it. Then read the whole piece once for flow.
- Run every line through three questions. Can the reader picture it? Can it be proven true or false? Could a competitor write the same sentence? Pass all three or rewrite from the facts.
- Ration the punch: one or two punchy moments per piece, usually the headline. Keep the rest plain, specific and declarative.
- Benefits over features. Specifics over vagueness ("Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" beats "Save time"). Customer words over company words. One idea per section.
- Claims must be supportable. When one is not, leave it out, say why, offer the closest true version and name the evidence that would back it (a review export, a certificate, a written guarantee). Mark gaps as [NEED: proof] instead of filling them.
- Never invent customers, quotes, testimonials, statistics, ratings, prices or results.
- Before a full rewrite, decide who is writing (the founder, the registered manager), what they know cold, and what they would cut. Let the part they know best run longest. Never invent their anecdotes.

## Banned patterns: rewrite on sight

Structures
- Contrast reveals: "It's not X, it's Y." "Not because X. Because Y." "Not just X, but Y." State Y directly with its reason.
- Negation lists: "No X, no Y, no Z." Say what does happen. One plain absence beside the CTA ("No card required") is fine.
- Trailing pile-ons: a full claim, then a comma and a run of restating clauses. End the sentence at the claim.
- Self-answered questions and colon reveals: "The result? 3x faster." "The best part: it learns." Write it as a sentence. Real FAQ questions are fine, and so is a question in the reader's own voice followed by what to do ("Need a carer this week? Call 023...").
- Dramatic fragments: "Simple. Fast. Done." One fragment per section at most.
- Reflexive threes. Use as many items as the facts support; two often beats three.
- False agency: "the data tells us", "the decision emerges", "the culture shifts". Name the person who acted.
- Passive voice that hides the actor ("Mistakes were made"). Put the actor first.
- Narrator from a distance: "People tend to..." "Nobody designed this." Put the reader in the scene with "you".
- Wh- openers used as a crutch: "What makes this hard is..." becomes "The constraint is...", or better, the constraint itself.
- Lazy extremes (every, always, never, everyone, nobody) doing vague work. Use the specific case.
- Wisdom-shaped lines, quotables and tidy closers: "Speed is a feature." "Because your team deserves better." End on a fact, the offer or the CTA.
- Self-narration and meta-commentary: "Here's why this matters." "Let me walk you through..." Delete it.

Phrases
- Openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "Whether you're X or Y", "Imagine...", "When it comes to", "Here's the thing", "The truth is", "Let's dive in".
- Marketing filler: "Say goodbye to", "Unlock the power of", "X, reimagined", "Take it to the next level", "Transform the way you work", "game-changer", "Join thousands of happy customers", "Everything you need".
- Vocabulary: delve, tapestry, testament, realm, embark, beacon, paradigm, synergy, myriad, leverage (as a verb), utilise, seamless, robust, streamline, empower, elevate, cutting-edge, innovative, world-class, best-in-class, holistic, journey (unless literal). Replace the word with the fact it stands for: "robust" becomes "99.9% uptime over two years".
- Adverbs and hedges: really, truly, genuinely, simply, actually, literally, deeply, incredibly, just. Cut them.
- Emphasis crutches: "Let that sink in", "Full stop", "Make no mistake", "Read that again".

Punctuation and format
- No em dashes in headlines, subheads, ads, posts, captions or subject lines, and at most one or two in long copy. Use a full stop, comma or colon.
- No exclamation marks in body copy. No emoji as bullets in business copy. Sentence case for headings unless the brand says otherwise.
- Vary sentence length on purpose. When three sentences in a row match in length, break one. Vary how paragraphs end.

## By format

Web pages
- Above the fold: a specific headline carrying the main value, a subhead of one or two sentences, and one primary CTA that says what they get ("Book a free care assessment" beats "Submit").
- Then proof, the problem, the outcome, how it works in three or four steps, objections and FAQ, and a final CTA with risk reversal.
- A landing page carries one message and one CTA, with the headline matched to the ad or post that sent the visitor.
- Weak CTAs to replace: Submit, Sign up, Learn more, Click here, Get started. Formula: action verb plus what they get.

Social posts (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok)
- The first line earns the rest: a specific fact, number, change or story. A hook formula is a shape to fill with real detail.
- Write in paragraphs and break lines where a person would pause. No broetry, meaning one short sentence per line all the way down.
- No engagement bait ("Agree?", "Thoughts?"), no manufactured vulnerability, no emoji bullets.
- Each post works on its own. External links in the post body can cut reach on some platforms; if reach drops, test the link in the first comment. A few relevant hashtags go at the end.
- For local businesses, name the town and area, the service and the way to act (call, book, apply).

Ads
- Specs: Meta primary text shows about 125 characters before "more" and headlines about 40; Google search headlines are 30 characters (up to 15) and descriptions 90 (up to 4). Check every line fits.
- Put audience knowledge into the creative: their words for the problem, the identity they recognise ("for carers in Southampton"), the outcome they want.
- Test the concept first, then the hook, the visual, the body and the CTA. Each test names its metric, its comparison and the expected direction.
- Caption every video ad. The first three seconds carry the hook with the sound off.

Emails
- One email, one job, one main CTA. Give value before the ask.
- Subject lines: clear over clever, specific, 40 to 60 characters, no fake "Re:" or "Fwd:", no em dashes. Preview text adds to the subject and never repeats it.
- Open with the reason you are writing. Cut "I hope this email finds you well", "Just checking in" and "Feel free to reach out". End with one specific action.
- Length: 50 to 125 words for transactional mail, 150 to 300 for educational, 300 to 500 for a story.

Job adverts and recruitment posts
- Lead with the role and the place. Then the facts candidates decide on: pay or a pay range where known, hours and patterns, training, progression, required checks (DBS, right to work, driving) and exactly how to apply, with the URL and phone number.
- Use proof the employer can stand behind, such as a published regulator rating, only when it is current and verifiable.
- No slogans about kindness, passion or family. Describe an ordinary day in the job.

SEO and AI search
- Title tags: unique, the main query near the front, about 50 to 60 characters, aligned with the H1. Meta descriptions: unique, about 150 to 160 characters, with the value and a reason to click.
- Write for extraction: lead each section with the direct answer, keep key answer passages to roughly 40 to 60 words, phrase headings the way people ask, use tables for comparisons and numbered steps for processes, one idea per paragraph.
- For local services, put the service and the town in titles and headings, keep the name, address and phone number identical everywhere, keep the Google Business Profile current and earn real reviews.
- Never stuff keywords, and never fake freshness, authors or reviews.

## Check before delivering

1. Search the draft for "not ", "isn't", "no ", "without", "?", ":" and "—" in short copy, and fix every banned pattern each hit reveals.
2. Read every sentence over 20 words. More than two commas usually means a pile-on.
3. Count fragments, threes, adverbs and filler words per section.
4. Apply the swap test to the headline, subhead and each section heading.
5. Read it aloud and rewrite anything that sounds like a press release or a LinkedIn post.
6. Score it from 1 to 10 on directness, rhythm, trust in the reader, sounding human and density. Below 35 out of 50, revise.
7. Fix from the facts. Swapping synonyms creates new tells.
